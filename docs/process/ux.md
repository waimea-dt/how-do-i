# User Experience (UX)

Usability is about whether a system *works*. User experience (UX) is about how using it *feels*. A product can be technically usable but still frustrating, confusing, or unpleasant - good UX means the whole interaction feels smooth, satisfying, and right for the user.

UX design considers everything a person encounters when using a product: the visual design, the words on screen, how long things take, what happens when something goes wrong, and whether the end result matches what they were hoping for.

## What Makes a Good User Experience?

Good UX comes down to a few core principles. You don't need to master all of them at once - but keeping them in mind during design makes a real difference.

> [!TIP]
> If you want to read more about good UX and the principles to follow, see [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/), or this article from [UX Design Institute](https://www.uxdesigninstitute.com/blog/ux-design-principles-2026/)

# UX Core Principles


## <i data-lucide="user-round"></i> Know Your User

> Design for real people, not yourself.

The single biggest UX mistake is assuming your users think like you do. Different people have different goals, skills, expectations, and patience levels.

**Consider:**
- Who is actually going to use this? (age, experience, context)
- What are they trying to achieve - and how quickly do they want to do it?
- What devices and environments will they be using it on?

User personas and user testing are key tools for answering these questions.

---

## <i data-lucide="map"></i> Clear User Journeys

> Know the paths users will take through your system.

A user journey is the sequence of steps someone takes to complete a goal. Every journey should have a clear start, a logical flow, and a satisfying end - without dead ends or unnecessary detours.

**Consider:**
- What is the most common task a user will perform? Is it easy to do?
- Are there points where users might get lost or give up?
- Does the layout guide users naturally towards the next step?

---

## <i data-lucide="layout-dashboard"></i> Familiar Layouts

> Don't make users relearn where things are.

People build mental models of how interfaces work based on everything they've used before. Following familiar conventions - navigation at the top, primary actions prominent, destructive actions away from common ones - reduces friction.

**Consider:**
- Does the layout follow conventions users already know?
- Is the navigation consistent across all pages?
- Are interactive elements (buttons, links, inputs) clearly distinguishable from static content?

---

## <i data-lucide="timer"></i> Respect User's Time

> Every extra click, wait, or step costs goodwill.

Users abandon products that feel slow or make them work too hard. Reducing unnecessary steps and keeping interactions snappy makes a product feel polished and respectful.

**Consider:**
- Can common tasks be completed in as few steps as possible?
- Are loading times and delays minimised and communicated clearly?
- Are forms and inputs as short as they can be while still collecting what's needed?

---

## <i data-lucide="message-circle"></i> Clear, Helpful Language

> Every word in your interface is part of the UX.

Labels, button text, error messages, and instructions all shape how confident and in-control a user feels. Vague or technical language creates uncertainty; clear language builds trust.

**Consider:**
- Are labels and button text specific and action-oriented? ("Save changes" not "Submit")
- Is the reading level appropriate for the target audience?
- Do error messages explain what went wrong and how to fix it?

---

## <i data-lucide="smile"></i> Design for Emotion

> How should using this make people feel?

Great UX isn't just about removing frustration - it's about creating moments that feel good. Small details like smooth animations, encouraging feedback, and a friendly tone all contribute to a positive emotional experience.

**Consider:**
- Does the product feel welcoming and approachable, or cold and clinical?
- Are success states acknowledged? (e.g. a confirmation message after completing a task)
- Does the visual design match the tone and audience? (e.g. playful for a kids' app, clean and professional for a business tool)

---

## <i data-lucide="test-tube-diagonal"></i> Test With Real Users

> Your assumptions will be wrong. Testing shows you where.

No amount of planning replaces watching a real person try to use your product. Even a short usability test with two or three users reveals problems that designers can't see because they're too close to their own work.

**Consider:**
- Have people outside the design team tried to use it?
- Were they able to complete core tasks without help?
- What confused or frustrated them - and how can the design be adjusted?
